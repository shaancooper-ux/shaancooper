Selected projects

My work with the student team at the Experimental Rocket Propulsion Lab.

Feb–Apr 2026 · Team hot-fire in October

Draco: test stand rebuild

I was a project lead on the rebuild of our damaged test stand. My main responsibilities were the fluids layout, tank enclosure CAD, and pressure-loss calculations for the bang-bang pressurization system.

We moved the run lines and Venturis lower to limit liquid oxygen boil-off and brought the tanks inside the frame to simplify setup. I helped assemble the plumbing and frame, cut and drill thrust-structure beams, and set up the stand for testing.

Setup went from about five hours to three. On October 16, 2026, our team fired a 2,000 lbf engine on the rebuilt stand.
Jan–Mar 2026 · Unbuilt design study

Murph: mobile feed system

I was a lead on the fluids design for Murph. A teammate and I designed the fluid components together, and I completed roughly half of the CAD. Our goal was a trailer-mounted system that could accommodate new engines without a major rebuild between test campaigns.

We designed for cryogenic service, throttle control, larger tanks, and higher flow. Under optimistic sizing assumptions, the upper target was a 10,000 lbf engine. The fluids design and CAD were mostly complete when our team shifted to Draco; Murph was never built or tested.

Thrust structure analysis

Using the team’s existing CAD, I simulated how the vertical thrust structure would deform under twice the expected engine load. The model included AR500 steel and 316 stainless steel, with an assumed bolt-tightening torque of 12 ft·lbf.

The contour shows predicted total deformation for that load case.

Aug 2025–present · Python

Hot-fire data analysis

I wrote a Python script to isolate the active burn using thrust thresholds, then compared measured thrust and chamber pressure with predictions. With the calculated propellant-line settings, chamber pressure came close to the target; thrust was slightly below prediction.

The plots show measured hot-fire data. The tank-pressure trends were consistent with one tank having more gas space above the liquid as the propellants drained.

How the set-pressure calculator works

For each propellant line, I subtract the Venturi throat pressure from its inlet pressure to get ΔP. I convert that difference from psi to pascals, then calculate mass flow:

ṁ = (CdA)Venturi √(2ρ ΔP)

The Venturi’s effective flow area, (CdA)Venturi, comes from calibration in earlier tests. With area in m² and the density of the fluid flowing through the Venturi in kg/m³ (water for a water run), the result is mass flow in kg/s.

The calculator then uses that flow and the effective flow area of the propellant line to estimate the pressure loss upstream of the chamber. Adding that loss to the target chamber pressure gives the line’s set pressure:

Pset = Pchamber,target + (ṁ / CdA)² / (2ρ)

Here, ṁ is the line’s mass flow rate, CdA is its effective flow area, and ρ is the propellant density. The result is the upstream pressure setting for that line.
